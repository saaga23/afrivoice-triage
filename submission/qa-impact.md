# Judge Q&A — Real-World Impact (20% weight)

## Q1: Your README claims "Healthcare triage for 1B+ Africans." Isn't that a classic overclaim? How many people can this actually serve today?

The 1B+ figure is the size of the problem, not our serviceable market today — a patient population that accesses care through speech, often code-switched, in low-literacy and low-bandwidth settings. What we can honestly claim today is the eight languages the pipeline supports (English, Swahili, Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Pidgin, Shona, Kinyarwanda), which together cover hundreds of millions of speakers. And even within those, our N=120 real-speech benchmark (`docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5) shows the product serves Hausa (0.371 WER), Pidgin (0.390), and Swahili (0.400) speakers well, while Igbo (0.881) and Shona (0.885) need a fallback or fine-tune before deployment. We published those uncomfortable per-language numbers rather than averaging them away — the 1B+ framing is the addressable need, and the per-language data is the honest map of where we actually deliver on it.

## Q2: This is a hackathon prototype. Has a single real patient or clinician ever used it? If not, how can you claim real-world impact?

No real patient has used it — that is the honest answer, and we don't pretend otherwise. What exists is a live, deployed product (https://app-phi-one-ah0g1z34ov.vercel.app) with the full workflow a pilot would exercise: consent modal → voice symptom report → urgency tier → triage handoff card. The impact claim is grounded in a measured, documented failure of the status quo rather than in user counts: in our benchmark, Whisper large-v3 silently deleted the entire Hausa segment of an urgent-care utterance, meaning the symptom never reached the agent — a concrete, reproducible patient-safety hazard in any English-first voice tool. The credible path to measured impact is a clinic pilot, which is exactly the shape of our continuation plan for the Sahara Switch Africa Challenge starting Oct 1.

## Q3: "Measurable outcomes" — what metrics would you actually track in deployment, and can you point to any measured outcome you already have?

The measured outcome we have today is on the input layer, because that is where the product's risk lives: WER per language on 120 real in-the-wild AfriSwitch utterances, with all three models on the same samples (Sahara 0.603 / Whisper 0.692 / XLS-R 0.853 overall). For a clinic deployment, the outcome metrics would be intake time per patient (baseline vs. with the handoff card), emergency-escalation recall (did every true emergency in a reviewed sample set get flagged — our deterministic keyword net exists to keep this near 1.0 across 8 languages), and language-mix coverage (share of patients served in their actual language mix vs. forced into English). None of the deployment metrics exist yet because there has been no deployment; we built the benchmark and reproducibility infrastructure (`/bench`, `public/data/*.jsonl`) precisely so those numbers can be measured, not asserted.

## Q4: Who exactly uses this, and when? Walk me through a real moment of use.

A community clinic or telehealth intake queue in Lagos, Nairobi, or Abuja: a patient opens the app on a basic smartphone, accepts the consent modal, and says in their natural mix — "Ina dauwa da zazzaɓar jiki… my temperature is very high." Sahara transcribes both language spans, the LangGraph agent classifies intent and assigns an urgency tier, and two things come out: a Sahara TTS voice reply for the patient, and a structured triage handoff card (urgency + symptom summary + recommended next step) for the clinician. The patient is the primary user at the moment of symptom onset, before or instead of walking into a crowded intake line; the clinician or telehealth provider is the secondary user receiving standardized intake. The "when" is specifically the pre-consultation gap — the queue where patients are currently triaged by whoever is free, in whatever language the front desk happens to speak.

## Q5: What happens without this tool? Clinics already have human intake — isn't a bilingual nurse better than your app in every way?

A bilingual nurse is better — when one exists, is free, and speaks the patient's particular language mix. The status quo is that intake queues are long, staff are scarce, and patients are routinely forced into English or a dominant local language that isn't theirs, losing symptom detail exactly where it matters. Without code-switch-tolerant triage, the technology fallback is English-first ASR — and we measured what that does: Whisper large-v3 silently deleted a Hausa fever complaint, and scored 0.892–0.959 WER on Hausa/Yoruba code-switching versus Sahara's 0.371–0.688. This tool doesn't replace the nurse; it gets the symptom accurately out of the patient's mouth and onto the clinician's desk as a handoff card, so scarce human time is spent on care, not on translation and re-asking. And the emergency keyword net escalates danger symptoms in 8 languages even when no English is spoken at all.

## Q6: Your own benchmark shows Sahara at 0.88 WER on Igbo and Shona — effectively unusable. Doesn't that gut your "hundreds of millions of speakers" claim and show the tool fails exactly the marginalized populations you say you serve?

It partially does, and we said so in the submission answers ourselves. The eight-language claim describes the interface and safety-net coverage — the emergency keyword net, consent flow, and agent all operate across en/sw/yo/ha/ig/pcm/sn/rw — but the ASR quality is honestly uneven: strong on Hausa/Pidgin/Swahili (0.37–0.40 WER), weak on Igbo/Shona (0.88). Notably, on Igbo and Shona the open-source Whisper is stronger (0.668/0.773), so the per-language routing mitigation already has measured evidence behind it. The honest position is that AfriVoice is deployable today for three language communities covering well over 100M speakers, and the per-language benchmark is the map that tells us — and any deploying clinic — where a fallback or fine-tune is required first. Publishing that map instead of hiding it is the difference between an impact claim and marketing.

## Q7: Low-literacy, low-bandwidth, basic smartphones — yet your system needs a live commercial API with a 7.4-second round trip. How does this survive a rural clinic with 2G?

That is a genuine tension and we don't wave it away. Three mitigations exist today: the architecture permits an offline Whisper fallback for low-connectivity sites (planned, not implemented, and it would ship with a visible accuracy caveat given the measured WER gap); the TTS path has AbortSignal timeouts and a 12-second cap with text-only fallback, so a provider stall never hangs the chat; and audio is sent once per utterance, not streamed continuously, which suits intermittent connectivity better than streaming designs. What we won't claim is that 2G is solved — Sahara's ~7.4 s/utterance API latency is acceptable on 3G+, and true offline deployment is on the continuation roadmap ("on-device deployment for low-bandwidth clinics" in the README). The privacy-first design (zero persistent storage) at least removes the data-dependency problem: nothing needs to sync.

## Q8: You claim scalability to fintech, legal, and agri. Isn't that speculative slide-ware? What in the architecture actually transfers?

The transferable part is measured and architectural, not aspirational: the pipeline is voice in → code-switch-tolerant ASR with per-language hints → agent classification → structured output card → voice reply, plus a deterministic domain-keyword safety net. Swap "symptom report / urgency" for "transaction dispute / fraud urgency" or "land-rights question / escalation," and the STT layer, language selector, consent flow, emergency-net pattern, and handoff-card UX carry over unchanged — the domain-specific parts (intent taxonomy, keyword lists, next-step recommendations) are config, not core code. What does not transfer automatically is the benchmark: health WER numbers say nothing about agri terminology, so each vertical needs its own per-language evaluation — which is why the benchmark runner and methodology (`/bench`, `public/data/`, `scripts/kaggle-benchmark.py`) are built to be reused. The fintech/legal/agri mention is the continuation plan for the Sahara Switch Africa Challenge, explicitly framed as future work, not shipped capability.

## Q9: Every response says "not a diagnosis, see a professional." So what does the tool actually accomplish that a poster saying "go to the clinic" doesn't?

Triage is not diagnosis — deciding *how urgently* and *where* a patient needs care is the high-volume problem in under-resourced health systems, and it's what the tool automates. Concretely it does four things a poster can't: it captures the symptom in the patient's own language mix (where English-first ASR provably loses entire symptom segments); it assigns a four-level urgency tier (low/moderate/high/emergency) so a chest-pain patient doesn't wait behind a rash; it produces a structured handoff card so the clinician starts with a standardized summary instead of from zero; and its emergency keyword net escalates danger words in 8 languages deterministically, independent of classifier confidence. The "see a professional" framing is an ethics feature (`docs/ETHICS.md`), not a limitation — the value is routing and prioritization, which is exactly the intake bottleneck clinics face.

## Q10: You store nothing — no patient records, no history. Doesn't that make the tool useless for continuity of care and impossible to ever measure real health outcomes?

It's a deliberate privacy-first design decision for a hackathon-stage product handling health voice data: audio and transcripts live in volatile memory only and are unrecoverable after the session (`docs/ETHICS.md`). The cost is real — no longitudinal record, no outcome tracking from the app itself. But the triage handoff card is designed precisely for this: it is the artifact that crosses into the clinic's own record system, where continuity of care properly belongs and where consent and health-data governance already exist. For outcome measurement, the honest answer is that a pilot would track metrics at the clinic level (intake time, escalation recall), not by silently retaining patient audio. We chose to ship something that can be put in front of patients without a data-protection review failing on day one; persistent, consented records are a deployment-time integration decision, not something a prototype should improvise.

## Q11: N=120 and N=6 pilots, synthetic TTS audio with disclosed circularity, zero field data. Why should a judge score impact highly when the evidence is this thin?

Because the evidence we do have is honest, reproducible, and targeted at the exact claim we make. We don't claim clinical outcomes; we claim that code-switched African speech breaks general ASR in ways that are dangerous for triage, and that Sahara measurably reduces that failure — that claim is backed by 120 real, in-the-wild test-split utterances with all three models on the same samples, per-sample JSONL published, and the failed first run documented rather than buried. The N=6 synthetic set's TTS circularity is disclosed in the benchmark report itself, and we explicitly designate the real-data run as the more trustworthy evidence. Impact scoring at hackathon stage should weigh whether the team correctly identified a real need, measured the gap rather than asserting it, and built the infrastructure to measure outcomes in a pilot — on all three, the repo speaks for itself (`docs/BENCHMARK.md`, `public/data/`, `/bench`).

## Q12: Sahara pricing isn't public and the API went down for hours during your own benchmarking. How can a cost-sensitive African health system depend on a proprietary, single-vendor pipeline?

Both facts are stated in our own benchmark report (§6): pricing is "Contact Intron," and we hit a multi-hour Intron-side outage mid-run. The mitigations are architectural and measured: the agent layer is decoupled from the ASR vendor — the per-language hint, timeouts, and fallback patterns mean Whisper or a fine-tuned XLS-R can slot in as primary or fallback, and we have per-language WER for all three models on identical samples to make that substitution an informed, quantified trade-off rather than a guess (e.g., Whisper is already the better choice for Igbo/Shona). On cost, the honest answer is that vendor pricing is an open question for scale — but the per-language benchmark doubles as the procurement document: it tells a health system exactly which languages justify a paid API and which can run on free open-source models today. Vendor risk is real; we chose to make it visible and routed-around rather than pretend it away.
