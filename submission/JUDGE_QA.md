# Judge Q&A — Real-World Impact (20% weight)

## Q1: Your README claims "Healthcare triage for 1B+ Africans." Isn't that a classic overclaim? How many people can this actually serve today?

The 1B+ figure is the size of the problem, not our serviceable market today — a patient population that accesses care through speech, often code-switched, in low-literacy and low-bandwidth settings. What we can honestly claim today is the eight languages the pipeline supports (English, Swahili, Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Pidgin, Shona, Kinyarwanda), which together cover hundreds of millions of speakers. And even within those, our N=120 real-speech benchmark (`docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5) shows the product serves Hausa (0.371 WER), Pidgin (0.390), and Swahili (0.400) speakers well, while Igbo (0.881) and Shona (0.885) need a fallback or fine-tune before deployment. We published those uncomfortable per-language numbers rather than averaging them away — the 1B+ framing is the addressable need, and the per-language data is the honest map of where we actually deliver on it.

## Q2: This is a hackathon prototype. Has a single real patient or clinician ever used it? If not, how can you claim real-world impact?

No real patient has used it — that is the honest answer, and we don't pretend otherwise. What exists is a live, deployed product (https://app-phi-one-ah0g1z34ov.vercel.app) with the full workflow a pilot would exercise: consent modal → voice symptom report → urgency tier → triage handoff card. The impact claim is grounded in a measured, documented failure of the status quo rather than in user counts: in our benchmark, Whisper large-v3 silently deleted the entire Hausa segment of an urgent-care utterance, meaning the symptom never reached the agent — a concrete, reproducible patient-safety hazard in any English-first voice tool. The credible path to measured impact is a clinic pilot, which is exactly the shape of our continuation plan for the Sahara Switch Africa Challenge starting Oct 1.

## Q3: "Measurable outcomes" — what metrics would you actually track in deployment, and can you point to any measured outcome you already have?

The measured outcome we have today is on the input layer, because that is where the product's risk lives: WER per language on 120 real in-the-wild AfriSwitch utterances, with all three models on the same samples (Sahara 0.603 / Whisper 0.692 / XLS-R 0.853 overall). For a clinic deployment, the outcome metrics would be intake time per patient (baseline vs. with the handoff card), emergency-escalation recall (did every true emergency in a reviewed sample set get flagged — our deterministic keyword net exists to keep this near 1.0 across 8 languages), and language-mix coverage (share of patients served in their actual language mix vs. forced into English). None of the deployment metrics exist yet because there has been no deployment; we built the benchmark and reproducibility infrastructure (`/bench`, `public/data/*.jsonl`) precisely so those numbers can be measured, not asserted.

## Q4: Who exactly uses this, and when? Walk me through a real moment of use.

A community clinic or telehealth intake queue in Lagos, Nairobi, or Abuja: a patient opens the app on a basic smartphone, accepts the consent modal, and says in their natural mix — "Ina dauwa da zazzaɓar jiki… my temperature is very high." Sahara transcribes both language spans, the LangGraph agent classifies intent and assigns an urgency tier, and two things come out: a Sahara TTS voice reply for the patient, and a structured triage handoff card (urgency + symptom summary + recommended next step) for the clinician. The patient is the primary user at the moment of symptom onset, before or instead of walking into a crowded intake line; the clinician or telehealth provider is the secondary user receiving standardized intake. The "when" is specifically the pre-consultation gap — the queue where patients are currently triaged by whoever is free, in whatever language the front desk happens to speak.

## Q5: What happens without this tool? Clinics already have human intake — isn't a bilingual nurse better than your app in every way?

A bilingual nurse is better — when one exists, is free, and speaks the patient's particular language mix. The status quo is that intake queues are long, staff are scarce, and patients are routinely forced into English or a dominant local language that isn't theirs, losing symptom detail exactly where it matters. Without code-switch-tolerant triage, the technology fallback is English-first ASR — and we measured what that does: Whisper large-v3 silently deleted a Hausa fever complaint, and scored 0.892–0.959 WER on Hausa/Yoruba code-switching versus Sahara's 0.371–0.688. This tool doesn't replace the nurse; it gets the symptom accurately out of the patient's mouth and onto the clinician's desk as a handoff card, so scarce human time is spent on care, not on translation and re-asking. And the emergency keyword net escalates danger symptoms in 8 languages even when no English is spoken at all.

## Q6: Your own benchmark shows Sahara at 0.88 WER on Igbo and Shona — effectively unusable. Doesn't that gut your "hundreds of millions of speakers" claim and show the tool fails exactly the marginalized populations you say you serve?

It partially does, and we said so in the submission answers ourselves. The eight-language claim describes the interface and safety-net coverage — the emergency keyword net, consent flow, and agent all operate across en/sw/yo/ha/ig/pcm/sn/rw — but the ASR quality is honestly uneven: strong on Hausa/Pidgin/Swahili (0.37–0.40 WER), weak on Igbo/Shona (0.88). Notably, on Igbo and Shona the open-source Whisper is stronger (0.668/0.773), so the per-language routing mitigation already has measured evidence behind it. The honest position is that AfriVoice is deployable today for three language communities covering well over 100M speakers, and the per-language benchmark is the map that tells us — and any deploying clinic — where a fallback or fine-tune is required first. Publishing that map instead of hiding it is the difference between an impact claim and marketing.

## Q7: Low-literacy, low-bandwidth, basic smartphones — yet your system needs a live commercial API with a 7.4-second round trip. How does this survive a rural clinic with 2G?

That is a genuine tension and we don't wave it away. Three mitigations exist today: the architecture permits an offline Whisper fallback for low-connectivity sites (planned, not implemented, and it would ship with a visible accuracy caveat given the measured WER gap); the TTS path has AbortSignal timeouts and a 12-second cap with text-only fallback, so a provider stall never hangs the chat; and audio is sent once per utterance, not streamed continuously, which suits intermittent connectivity better than streaming designs. What we won't claim is that 2G is solved — Sahara's ~7.4 s/utterance API latency is acceptable on 3G+, and true offline deployment is on the continuation roadmap ("on-device deployment for low-bandwidth clinics" in the README). The privacy-first design (zero persistent storage) at least removes the data-dependency problem: nothing needs to sync.

## Q8: You claim scalability to fintech, legal, and agri. Isn't that speculative slide-ware? What in the architecture actually transfers?

The transferable part is measured and architectural, not aspirational: the pipeline is voice in → code-switch-tolerant ASR with per-language hints → agent classification → structured output card → voice reply, plus a deterministic domain-keyword safety net. Swap "symptom report / urgency" for "transaction dispute / fraud urgency" or "land-rights question / escalation," and the STT layer, language selector, consent flow, emergency-net pattern, and handoff-card UX carry over unchanged — the domain-specific parts (intent taxonomy, keyword lists, next-step recommendations) are config, not core code. What does not transfer automatically is the benchmark: health WER numbers say nothing about agri terminology, so each vertical needs its own per-language evaluation — which is why the benchmark runner and methodology (`/bench`, `public/data/`, `scripts/kaggle-benchmark.py`) are built to be reused. The fintech/legal/agri mention is the continuation plan for the Sahara Switch Africa Challenge, explicitly framed as future work, not shipped capability.

## Q9: Every response says "not a diagnosis, see a professional." So what does the tool actually accomplish that a poster saying "go to the clinic" doesn't?

Triage is not diagnosis — deciding *how urgently* and *where* a patient needs care is the high-volume problem in under-resourced health systems, and it's what the tool automates. Concretely it does four things a poster can't: it captures the symptom in the patient's own language mix (where English-first ASR provably loses entire symptom segments); it assigns a four-level urgency tier (low/moderate/high/emergency) so a chest-pain patient doesn't wait behind a rash; it produces a structured handoff card so the clinician starts with a standardized summary instead of from zero; and its emergency keyword net escalates danger words in 8 languages deterministically, independent of classifier confidence. The "see a professional" framing is an ethics feature (`docs/ETHICS.md`), not a limitation — the value is routing and prioritization, which is exactly the intake bottleneck clinics face.

## Q10: You store nothing — no patient records, no history. Doesn't that make the tool useless for continuity of care and impossible to ever measure real health outcomes?

It's a deliberate privacy-first design decision for a hackathon-stage product handling health voice data: audio and transcripts live in volatile memory only and are unrecoverable after the session (`docs/ETHICS.md`). The cost is real — no longitudinal record, no outcome tracking from the app itself. But the triage handoff card is designed precisely for this: it is the artifact that crosses into the clinic's own record system, where continuity of care properly belongs and where consent and health-data governance already exist. For outcome measurement, the honest answer is that a pilot would track metrics at the clinic level (intake time, escalation recall), not by silently retaining patient audio. We chose to ship something that can be put in front of patients without a data-protection review failing on day one; persistent, consented records are a deployment-time integration decision, not something a prototype should improvise.

## Q11: N=120 and N=6 pilots, synthetic TTS audio with disclosed circularity, zero field data. Why should a judge score impact highly when the evidence is this thin?

Because the evidence we do have is honest, reproducible, and targeted at the exact claim we make. We don't claim clinical outcomes; we claim that code-switched African speech breaks general ASR in ways that are dangerous for triage, and that Sahara measurably reduces that failure — that claim is backed by 120 real, in-the-wild test-split utterances with all three models on the same samples, per-sample JSONL published, and the failed first run documented rather than buried. The N=6 synthetic set's TTS circularity is disclosed in the benchmark report itself, and we explicitly designate the real-data run as the more trustworthy evidence. Impact scoring at hackathon stage should weigh whether the team correctly identified a real need, measured the gap rather than asserting it, and built the infrastructure to measure outcomes in a pilot — on all three, the repo speaks for itself (`docs/BENCHMARK.md`, `public/data/`, `/bench`).

## Q12: Sahara pricing isn't public and the API went down for hours during your own benchmarking. How can a cost-sensitive African health system depend on a proprietary, single-vendor pipeline?

Both facts are stated in our own benchmark report (§6): pricing is "Contact Intron," and we hit a multi-hour Intron-side outage mid-run. The mitigations are architectural and measured: the agent layer is decoupled from the ASR vendor — the per-language hint, timeouts, and fallback patterns mean Whisper or a fine-tuned XLS-R can slot in as primary or fallback, and we have per-language WER for all three models on identical samples to make that substitution an informed, quantified trade-off rather than a guess (e.g., Whisper is already the better choice for Igbo/Shona). On cost, the honest answer is that vendor pricing is an open question for scale — but the per-language benchmark doubles as the procurement document: it tells a health system exactly which languages justify a paid API and which can run on free open-source models today. Vendor risk is real; we chose to make it visible and routed-around rather than pretend it away.

# Judge Q&A — Code-Switching Benchmark Quality (30%)

## Q1: N=120 (and N=6 for the synthetic pilot) — why should we treat this as a meaningful benchmark and not a demo with numbers?

N=120 is pilot-scale and we say so explicitly in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §1 ("Honest scope note"); it is not a full AfriSwitch evaluation, which has 650–1,877 test utterances per config. What makes it meaningful is the design, not the size: all three models ran on the *same* 120 utterances (20 per language × 6 configs) with identical `jiwer` normalization, so the comparison is paired, not apples-to-oranges. The headline gap is large relative to the noise: Sahara 0.603 vs Whisper 0.692 mean WER, a paired per-sample difference of −0.090 with a 95% CI of roughly [−0.163, −0.017] computed from the published per-sample JSONL — the interval excludes zero. What N=120 does *not* support is strong per-language claims (n=20 each), and we don't make them; per-language results are reported as directional. The N=6 synthetic set is framed as a qualitative pilot only, with the real-data run explicitly named "the more trustworthy evidence."

## Q2: Your synthetic samples were generated with Sahara TTS, then transcribed by Sahara ASR. Isn't that circular — Sahara recognizing its own voice?

Yes, and we disclose exactly that in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5 ("Circularity disclosure"): the 6 synthetic references were generated with Sahara TTS, so Sahara ASR has an inherent advantage on its own voice in the Section 2 numbers (0.347/0.450/0.604). We did it anyway for an engineering reason — we needed consented, de-identified, metadata-labeled health-domain audio with known references for the `/bench` demo and pipeline smoke tests — and each sample ships a JSON sidecar in `public/data/synthetic-samples/` labeling it synthetic. The mitigation is that we don't rest any conclusion on it: the decision-grade evidence is the N=120 run on real in-the-wild AfriSwitch podcast/YouTube speech, where no TTS circularity exists. A judge can safely ignore Section 2 and the product decision still stands on Section 5 alone.

## Q3: Sahara is Intron's commercial model and AfriSwitch is Intron's own dataset. How do you know Sahara wasn't trained on your exact benchmark samples? Isn't this a home-field advantage?

This is a fair concern and we can't fully rule it out — AfriSwitch is published by Intron Health (`intronhealth/AfriSwitch` on Hugging Face), and we evaluated on the official *test* split, but we have no visibility into Sahara v2's training data. Three things bound the risk. First, we used the official test split precisely because it is the community-agreed held-out partition; any published AfriSwitch benchmark faces the identical issue with Intron's own models. Second, Sahara's advantage is not uniform: Whisper *beats* it on Igbo (0.668 vs 0.881) and Shona (0.773 vs 0.885), which is not what trivial memorization would look like. Third, our product claim doesn't depend on Sahara being clean — the challenge is to compare Sahara against open-source options on code-switched speech, and we report the comparison as measured, with per-language wins and losses shown honestly in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5.

## Q4: You gave Sahara the correct per-language ASR hint (`use_language_asr_input`) but let Whisper fend for itself. Stacked deck?

Sahara got an oracle language ID (sw/yo/ha/ig/pcm/sn per config) while Whisper large-v3 ran its own built-in language identification — that asymmetry is real and disclosed in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5. We defend it on production-fidelity grounds: AfriVoice Triage knows the user's language from session context, so hinting the ASR mirrors actual deployment, and forcing Sahara to guess would benchmark a configuration we'd never ship. We also ran the no-hint variant — the Aug 5 attempt — and it collapsed (107/119 empty transcripts), which we disclosed rather than buried, so judges can see both conditions. Note the hint didn't rescue Sahara where it's weak: even with the correct hint it lost Igbo and Shona to Whisper. The cleanest framing: the comparison measures "each model configured the way this product would actually run it," not "each model under identical information."

## Q5: 8 of 120 Sahara samples returned empty transcripts and you scored them WER 1.0. Why is that fair, and what does a 6.7% silence rate mean for a triage product?

Scoring empty output as WER 1.0 is the conservative, worst-case choice — a silent transcript in triage means zero information captured, which is maximally bad, and penalizing it fully keeps us honest (Whisper and XLS-R returned text for all 120). The empties (`swahili-7`, `hausa-12`, `igbo-10`, `pidgin-2/6/12/17`, `shona-4`) were very short or noisy clips the API declined to transcribe; they are visible in `public/data/benchmark-afriswitch-sahara.jsonl`. Had we excluded them, Sahara's mean would drop to ~0.574, so the published 0.603 understates Sahara's accuracy on audio it accepts. The production implication is real, though: 8/120 silence on noisy in-the-wild audio is why the app has an emergency keyword net and an intent pipeline that degrades gracefully, and why the report flags the silent-deletion failure mode (Whisper dropped an entire Hausa segment in the synthetic pilot) as the patient-safety hazard it is.

## Q6: Whisper beats Sahara on Igbo and Shona, and Yoruba WER is 0.688 for Sahara. Your own numbers show Sahara is language-dependent — so why is Sahara the product choice?

Because the aggregate and the target deployment languages favor it, and we never claimed universality. Sahara leads overall (0.603 vs 0.692), wins Hausa (0.371 vs 0.892), Swahili (0.400 vs 0.525), and Yoruba (0.688 vs 0.959), essentially ties Pidgin (0.390 vs 0.338, the one clear Whisper win in West Africa), and loses Igbo and Shona — all reported per-language in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5 with the explicit statement that "neither model is universally best, and deployment language should drive the choice." The architecture reflects that: Sahara is the primary STT/TTS path, and the report notes a planned offline Whisper fallback, which doubles as the per-language escape hatch. Honest weakness: Sahara's Yoruba (0.688) and Igbo/Shona (~0.88) numbers are not good enough for unsupervised triage, which is one reason the agent layers an intent/urgency pipeline and emergency keyword net over raw ASR rather than trusting transcripts verbatim.

## Q7: Where are your confidence intervals and significance tests? A table of means with n=20 per language is not statistical rigor.

Correct — the report presents means and medians without inferential statistics, and that is a genuine gap we're not going to dress up. What the submission does provide is stronger than a p-value screenshot: the full per-sample paired data (`benchmark-afriswitch-sahara.jsonl`, `benchmark-afriswitch-opensource.jsonl`, `benchmark-afriswitch-xlsr-cpu.jsonl` in `public/data/`), so any judge can compute the statistics themselves in a few lines. We did exactly that during prep: the paired Sahara−Whisper difference is −0.090 WER (95% CI ≈ [−0.163, −0.017], excludes zero; Sahara wins 72/120 pairwise), and the overall Sahara mean is 0.603 with 95% CI ≈ [0.545, 0.660]. Per-language cells (n=20) have wide intervals — Pidgin's 0.390 vs 0.338 is not a statistically meaningful separation — and we treat those as directional in the writeup. Given more time, adding bootstrap CIs to `scripts/kaggle-benchmark.py` output is the first improvement we'd make.

## Q8: Could this benchmark be gamed? You picked the 20 samples per language, you ran the models, you wrote the report.

The specific gaming vectors are closed or disclosed. Sample selection was a deterministic prefix of the official AfriSwitch test split (first 20 per config), not a searched-for favorable subset, and the same 120 utterances went to all three models with one shared `jiwer` normalization — there was no per-model sample shopping. Every per-sample transcript, WER, and latency is published in `public/data/*.jsonl`, so a judge can recompute every number in the report (we verified: recomputed means match to rounding, 0.6025/0.6924/0.8534). Most importantly, we disclosed the runs that make us look bad: the invalid first attempt (107/119 empties without the language hint), the multi-hour Intron outage that corrupted a rerun, and the wav2vec2 GPU failure — the failed wav2vec2 GPU rows (all `wer: null`) are still sitting in `benchmark-afriswitch-opensource.jsonl` rather than deleted. A team gaming the benchmark would have quietly rerun and shown only clean numbers; we documented the mess in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5 "Run transparency."

## Q9: WER is a blunt metric for tonal, diacritic-rich languages. Your own report shows a correct Yoruba rewrite scored as an error. Why is WER the right yardstick?

WER is the yardstick because it's the challenge's required metric and the only one directly comparable across the three models and with published ASR literature — but the report itself flags its limits in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5: some "errors" are orthographic, not acoustic (Yoruba reference *"What? E mean e…"* → Sahara *"Kí ni? Ẹ̀ túmọ̀ sí ẹ́…"*, a correct but orthography-shifted rewrite that WER punishes). Our normalization (lowercase, punctuation stripped, whitespace collapsed via `jiwer`) removes case/punctuation noise but does not normalize diacritics or dialect spelling variants, so absolute WERs on Yoruba/Igbo are inflated for *all* models — note Whisper scores even worse there (0.959/0.668), so the bias doesn't manufacture Sahara's win. The mitigation we'd add with more time is CER and a diacritic-insensitive normalized WER, plus a small human-correctness audit on health-critical spans (symptom and urgency words), which is arguably the metric that matters for triage. Until then we report WER as specified and annotate its known failure mode.

## Q10: Your latency numbers mix three different execution environments — API round-trip for Sahara, Kaggle GPU for Whisper, CPU for XLS-R. Isn't that comparison meaningless?

The three latency regimes are different, but they reflect how each model would actually ship in this product, and the report labels each number with its environment: Sahara 7.4 s/utterance (API upload + polling, network-bound), Whisper 79.6 s (Kaggle GPU fp16 on real data; 31.8 s CPU int8 in the pilot), XLS-R 3.1 s (CPU fp32). We never claim Sahara is fastest — XLS-R is, by 2.4×, and we say so ("fastest model but the least accurate"). The synthetic-pilot section also carries the explicit caveat that Whisper/wav2vec2 ran on CPU and their latencies would drop on GPU, while WER is hardware-independent — which is the key point: hardware confounds *latency*, not the accuracy comparison that drives the model choice. For a voice triage UX, 7.4 s API latency is a real cost we acknowledge (it's listed under Sahara's cons), and streaming or a persistent connection is the obvious optimization. The honest summary: WER comparison is controlled; latency comparison is descriptive of deployment reality, and we present it as such.

## Q11: Your Kaggle kernels are private (`is_private: true`) and one script hardcodes a live API key. Can a judge actually reproduce your numbers today?

Partially, and we're candid about the friction. Everything except the hosted kernel is public: the exact kernel scripts (`scripts/kaggle-benchmark.py` for the synthetic 3-model run; `kaggle-benchmark/sahara-afriswitch-kaggle.py`, `opensource-afriswitch-kaggle.py`, `xlsr-afriswitch-cpu.py` for the N=120 runs), the 120 audio samples in HF `datasets` format under `public/data/afriswitch/`, and the resulting JSONL all ship in the repo, and the Sahara script even pulls the audio straight from GitHub raw. Re-running needs two things: a Sahara API key and a Kaggle (or local) environment — the repo `scripts/kaggle-benchmark.py` correctly reads `SAHARA_API_KEY` from the environment, but we acknowledge `kaggle-benchmark/sahara-afriswitch-kaggle.py` has a key hardcoded from the deadline crunch; that's a secret-hygiene mistake we intend to rotate and fix, not a reproducibility feature. The Kaggle kernels are private because they were run under a personal account (`abrahamsunday123/afrivoice-sahara-afriswitch-120`), but the scripts are verbatim what executed, and the run logs are kept in `kaggle-benchmark/output/`. So: independently verifiable from published artifacts — yes; one-click reproduction — no, and that's a fair knock.

## Q12: Your first N=120 run was invalid and a rerun hit an Intron outage. Why should judges trust the final numbers and not suspect you kept rerunning until you liked the result?

Because the failed runs are documented in the report itself (`docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5 "Run transparency") with their failure modes quantified — 107/119 empty transcripts from the missing language hint, and empty transcripts even on previously-working files during the Intron-side outage, verified by direct probe. A cherry-picker would have silently discarded those runs; instead we diagnosed *why* each failed (missing `use_language_asr_input`, provider outage, Kaggle CUDA kernel-image error for wav2vec2 GPU), fixed the cause, and disclosed the history. The failed artifacts weren't cleaned up either — the wav2vec2 GPU rows with `wer: null` remain in `benchmark-afriswitch-opensource.jsonl` next to Whisper's valid rows, which is why that file has 240 lines for 120 samples. The final numbers come from clean, post-recovery runs whose per-sample outputs are fully published and internally consistent (recomputed per-language and overall means match the report). Trust here rests on reproducible artifacts plus disclosed failures, not on our say-so.

# Judge Q&A — Product Quality & Fit (25%)

## Q1: Is this actually agentic, or is it just a transcription demo with keyword rules bolted on?

It is a real agent pipeline, and the code shows the structure: `src/lib/agent.ts` builds a LangGraph `StateGraph` with three nodes — `classify_intent` → `route_to_tool` → `generate_response` — with typed state (intent, urgency, toolCalls, triageSummary) flowing between them. The agent classifies intent into four categories (triage, booking, refill, general_support), routes to a labeled tool, assigns one of four urgency tiers, and emits a structured `TriageSummary` handoff card before Sahara TTS speaks the reply. The UI surfaces the reasoning: intent and urgency badges plus deduplicated, labeled tool calls, so a judge can watch the agent's steps rather than take our word for it. That said, we are honest about the depth: this is a focused triage agent, not an open-ended planner — the graph is deliberately linear because a safety-critical intake flow should be predictable, not exploratory.

## Q2: Your production deployment has no OpenAI API key. Doesn't that mean the live product is just canned rule-based responses?

Yes, and we disclose this rather than hide it: `createLLMClient()` in `agent.ts` returns null without a key and the graph falls through to rule-based intent classification and `ruleBasedResponse()`. The important nuance is that the safety-critical behavior never depended on the LLM in the first place: `computeUrgency()` and the multilingual emergency net are deterministic on every path, so urgency tiers and emergency escalation are identical with or without a key. What degrades without the LLM is conversational quality — intent classification falls back to keyword matching and replies become canned templates instead of natural, language-mixed responses. The mitigation is architectural, not aspirational: the LLM is an enhancement layer behind a try/catch, and any OpenAI-compatible key restores full behavior with zero code changes.

## Q3: Your "tool calling" — the booking tool doesn't actually book anything. Isn't that overclaiming?

Fair criticism, and we'd answer it directly: `route_to_tool` labels and routes intents (`triage`, `booking`) and records them in the `toolCalls` state channel, but the tools do not yet execute side effects — there is no scheduling backend behind booking. What we built and validated is the agentic routing layer: intent detection, tool selection, visible tool-call traces, and a deduplication reducer, which is the part this challenge asks us to demonstrate. We deliberately did not fake a booking confirmation, because a triage product that pretends to have scheduled an appointment is a safety liability. The honest framing is: the agent decides and routes today; wiring the tools to real clinic systems is integration work, not research.

## Q4: The triage "handoff card" is just the raw transcript truncated to 300 characters, and recommended_action only ever has two values. How is that a structured clinical handoff?

The structure is real but shallow, and we know where it stops. `TriageSummary` in `agent.ts` is a typed object — symptoms, urgency (four tiers), recommended_action, and a hardcoded `not_a_diagnosis: true` flag — rendered as a distinct card in the UI. Today the symptoms field carries the transcript excerpt rather than extracted symptom entities, and recommended_action resolves to `seek_immediate_care` or `collect_more_symptoms_then_advise`. The README's mention of self-care/telehealth/in-person recommendations reflects the LLM-path response text, not the structured enum, and we should be precise about that distinction. What the card proves is the contract: a machine-readable urgency tier plus summary that a clinician dashboard could consume — the extraction depth is the next iteration, and the schema already accommodates it.

## Q5: A "handoff" implies someone receives it. With zero persistent storage and no clinic integration, the card disappears when the session ends. Who does it hand off to?

Today, it hands off to the human in the loop at the screen — the card persists in the chat UI for the patient to show a clinician or read to a provider. The absence of persistence is a deliberate privacy decision documented in `docs/ETHICS.md` (volatile memory only, nothing written to disk), and for a hackathon handling health speech we chose the safe side of that tradeoff. The cost is real: there is no queue, no EHR push, no audit trail. The design intent is that the `TriageSummary` JSON is the integration artifact — a clinic system would POST it to an intake endpoint under the clinic's own consent and retention policies, which are decisions a deployer must own, not a demo.

## Q6: The agent asks "how long have you been feeling this way?" — but the next request starts a completely fresh state. Isn't the multi-turn conversation an illusion?

In the current deployment, yes, each `/api/chat` call constructs a new `VoiceAgent` and a new state with only the latest message (`route.ts` lines 27–34) — the clarifying question is asked but the follow-up answer arrives without prior context. This is a genuine product limitation we won't dress up: the LangGraph state model supports full message history (the `messages` channel is an append reducer designed for exactly that), but we did not wire client-side history into the request before the deadline. What works today is single-turn triage with correct urgency and escalation on each utterance independently — which, not coincidentally, is how the emergency safety net should behave anyway: escalation must never depend on remembering earlier turns.

## Q7: You claim the system "responds in the language mix the patient uses," but your rule-based fallback responses are English-only strings. Doesn't the live product reply in English to a Hausa speaker?

With the LLM configured, the system prompt explicitly instructs same-language and code-switched replies, and Sahara TTS voices the response in the patient's language. Without the key, the honest answer is that `ruleBasedResponse()` returns English templates, so the fallback path does not fully honor the language-mixing claim — TTS speaks an English reply to a Yoruba patient. We partially mitigated the highest-stakes case: the emergency template is short, imperative, and paired with a red EMERGENCY badge whose meaning survives a language barrier, and the multilingual keyword net ensures the *detection* side is fully eight-language even when the *response* side is not. Localizing the fallback templates into all eight languages is a small, well-scoped fix we would ship first.

## Q8: Your emergency net covers 8 languages, but general symptom detection only covers English, Swahili, Hausa, and Yoruba. What happens to a non-emergency Igbo or Shona patient?

They fall through to `general_support` intent and low urgency unless the LLM is active — `SYMPTOM_TERMS` in `agent.ts` covers en/sw/ha/yo plus English severity phrases, and `docs/ETHICS.md` states this scope explicitly. Compounding it, our N=120 real-speech benchmark shows Sahara's ASR is weakest exactly on Igbo (0.881 WER) and Shona (0.885), so those speakers face both weaker transcription and weaker keyword coverage. Our mitigation strategy is layered: the emergency net — the case where a miss is dangerous — covers all eight languages including Igbo (`obara`, `iku ume`) and Shona (`ropa`, `kupfuma`); the benchmark publishes the per-language weakness instead of averaging it away; and the submission names Igbo/Shona as the languages where deployment needs a fallback or fine-tune first. We'd rather lose points for a disclosed gap than gain them for a hidden one.

## Q9: The emergency net is substring matching — "no chest pain" triggers emergency. Isn't that a trivially gameable safety mechanism?

It has the known failure modes of keyword matching, including the negation false positive you describe, and ASCII-folding substring match can also over-trigger on partial words. Our defense is the direction of the error: in triage, over-escalation costs a clinician a glance; under-escalation costs a patient their life. Every clinical triage protocol makes the same asymmetry explicit, and the net's output is a recommendation to seek care — never a diagnosis or a dispatched ambulance — so the blast radius of a false positive is bounded. The architecture also doesn't rely on the net alone: with the LLM active, the classifier handles semantics and negation while the net acts as an independent floor, so the two mechanisms' errors are largely uncorrelated. Improving the net (word boundaries, negation windows) is easy; we would never ship the inverse tradeoff.

## Q10: What specifically did you build for low-literacy users, beyond claiming "voice-first" in the README?

Concretely: the primary input is holding a button and speaking — no reading or typing required to report symptoms; the reply arrives as Sahara TTS audio, not just text; urgency is communicated by color-coded badges (the red emergency state reads at a glance); and the consent flow degrades gracefully — declining voice still leaves a working text interface rather than a dead end. We also engineered for the connectivity these users actually have: a 12-second TTS timeout with text-only fallback and AbortSignal timeouts mean a stalled provider queue never hangs the chat, and ETHICS.md commits to basic smartphones and low-bandwidth connections. The honest residual gap is chrome, not core: the consent modal and language selector are still text, so a fully non-literate user needs assistance for first setup — a spoken consent flow is the obvious next step.

## Q11: Is the demo video a real product recording or a staged mock? And why is the YouTube link still pending?

It is a real screen recording of the live production deployment at the submitted Vercel URL — `public/demo-video/afrivoice-demo-final.mp4`, 58 seconds, narrated by Sahara TTS with burned-in subtitles for muted judging. It shows the actual flow: consent modal → a code-switched Swahili–English symptom report producing intent + urgency badges and the triage handoff card → an emergency utterance ("severe chest pain… cannot breathe") triggering the red emergency badge and seek-immediate-care guidance → the benchmark methodology page. Nothing is mocked; the processing times you see are real API round-trips, which is why the earlier longer cut exists alongside it. The YouTube link is pending purely as an upload errand before the 13:00 WAT deadline — the video file itself is in the repo and plays independently of any hosting.

## Q12: Walk me through how this fits a real clinic's intake workflow today — with 7.4s average latency, single-turn state, and no EHR integration, what actually works?

The realistic deployment shape today is a kiosk or intake-helper tablet: a patient speaks their complaint while waiting, and by the time they reach the desk there is an urgency badge and a summary card the nurse reads in seconds — the card standardizes the first question every intake asks anyway. Latency supports this: 7.4 s/utterance mean on the N=120 run is acceptable for a triage exchange (patients pause longer between sentences in person), and the emergency path does not wait on anything — the keyword net fires deterministically on the transcript. What does not exist yet — multi-turn memory, EHR write-back, a triage queue — is exactly the set of things that require a clinic partner's data governance decisions, which is why we built the integration contract (the `TriageSummary` JSON and the `/api/chat` response shape) rather than a pretend integration. The product claim we stand behind is narrow: it makes the symptom survive transcription and arrive prioritized, in the language the patient actually spoke.

# Judge Q&A — Technical Execution (15%)

## Q1: Walk us through the end-to-end architecture when a patient speaks into the app.

The browser records audio and POSTs base64 audio to `src/app/api/chat/route.ts`, a Next.js 14 API route (`force-dynamic`, `maxDuration = 60`). The route hands the audio to `VoiceAgent.processVoiceInput` in `src/lib/agent.ts`, which calls the Sahara client (`src/lib/sahara.ts`): upload to `infer.voice.intron.io/file/v1/upload` with a per-language ASR hint, then poll `/file/v1/status` every 3 s until `FILE_TRANSCRIBED`. The transcript enters a LangGraph `StateGraph` with three nodes — `classify_intent`, `route_to_tool`, `generate_response` — producing an intent, an urgency tier (low/moderate/high/emergency), and a structured `TriageSummary` handoff card. The response text is then synthesized by Sahara TTS and returned as a data URL alongside the JSON payload. Every layer has a degradation path: LLM failure falls through to rule-based responses, and TTS failure never blocks the text response.

## Q2: Your README headlines "Sahara 0.347 WER" but your own real-data benchmark says 0.603. Isn't the headline misleading?

The 0.347 figure is clearly sourced: it comes from the 6-sample synthetic pilot, and `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5 discloses the circularity explicitly — those references were generated with Sahara TTS, so Sahara ASR has an inherent advantage on its own voice. The same report presents the N=120 real AfriSwitch run (same 120 samples for all three models) as "the more trustworthy evidence": Sahara 0.603, Whisper 0.692, XLS-R 0.853. We kept the synthetic pilot because it covers health-domain phrasing and Kinyarwanda, which AfriSwitch does not, but we agree the honest headline is the real-data number, and the benchmark doc says so in writing. If we revised the README today we would lead with 0.603/0.692/0.853 and demote the pilot to a footnote.

## Q3: Sahara loses to Whisper on Igbo (0.881 vs 0.668) and Shona (0.885 vs 0.773). How do you justify Sahara as the primary STT?

We don't claim Sahara is universally best — the benchmark doc states that explicitly and says "deployment language should drive the choice." Sahara wins the overall WER (0.603 vs 0.692) and is dramatically better on Hausa (0.371 vs 0.892), Swahili (0.400 vs 0.525), and Yoruba (0.688 vs 0.959); Whisper's edge is concentrated in Igbo and Shona. Sahara also gives us STT and TTS in one managed API with zero model ops, which matters for a hackathon-scale team. Architecturally the Sahara dependency is isolated behind the `SaharaClient` class, so a per-language router (e.g., Whisper for Igbo/Shona) is a real future option, not a rewrite. The honest weakness: an Igbo- or Shona-dominant deployment today would get our worst transcription quality, and we'd flag that to any pilot partner.

## Q4: Why a rule-based agent instead of just using an LLM for triage?

It's actually a hybrid, and the layering is deliberate. Intent classification and free-text response use gpt-4o-mini when `OPENAI_API_KEY` is configured (`src/lib/agent.ts`), with deterministic keyword rules as fallback when the key is missing or the LLM call throws. But urgency classification — the safety-critical decision — is always rule-based (`computeUrgency`), even on the LLM path: an emergency keyword hit forces the `emergency` tier no matter what the model says. That is a safety invariant, not a limitation: LLMs are stochastic, and a triage system cannot have a nonzero probability of rating "I can't breathe" as low urgency because of a sampling artifact. Rules also give us deterministic behavior in low-connectivity deployments and zero marginal cost per request. The trade-off we accept is that the rule-based fallback responses are generic; the LLM path is where conversational quality lives.

## Q5: Your emergency "safety net" is substring matching. "No chest pain" contains "chest" — false emergency. And novel phrasings slip through. Defend this.

The criticism is fair on both counts, and we'd rather defend the direction than pretend the mechanism is sophisticated. In triage, the asymmetry matters: a false positive escalates a patient unnecessarily (costly but safe), while a false negative misses an emergency (dangerous), so a high-recall keyword net is the right failure bias for a first line of defense — and it runs on the raw transcript in microseconds across 8 languages with no API dependency. The negation case ("no chest pain") is a genuine known limitation of `detectEmergency` in `src/lib/agent.ts`, which does pure ASCII-folded substring matching with no negation or context handling. Mitigations we'd add next: a short negation window check, LLM-based urgency confirmation as a second stage (with the keyword net still able to escalate but the LLM allowed to de-escalate only with logged rationale), and a labeled eval set of emergency utterances per language to measure the net's recall directly. What we will not do is remove the deterministic net in favor of LLM-only detection — the whole point is a layer that cannot hallucinate.

## Q6: End-to-end latency of ~6–7 seconds is slow for a patient in distress. Where does the time go, and how would you cut it?

The dominant cost is the Sahara STT round trip: file upload plus 3-second-interval polling, measured at 7.4 s/utterance on real AfriSwitch audio and 8.7 s on the synthetic set (`docs/BENCHMARK.md` §2/§5). The LangGraph agent step is milliseconds on the rule-based path (a few hundred ms with the LLM), and Sahara TTS adds roughly 1–3 s but is capped by a hard 12 s budget and is non-blocking — the text triage response renders even if TTS stalls (`src/lib/agent.ts` comment: "a stalled TTS queue must not hold the triage response hostage"). For emergencies specifically, the urgency badge and seek-care guidance could be shown from the transcript alone before TTS completes; that ordering optimization is straightforward given the pipeline shape. Bigger reductions would come from a streaming/websocket STT endpoint (Sahara's file-upload API is inherently batch) and from pre-warming, but those depend on Intron's API surface more than on our code.

## Q7: What breaks first at 1,000 concurrent users?

Our own tier is fine: the app is fully stateless (a fresh `VoiceAgent` per request, no shared mutable state), so Vercel serverless scales horizontally and volatile memory per invocation is by design. The first real bottleneck is the Intron Sahara API — 1,000 concurrent uploads plus ~3 status polls per transcription would almost certainly hit rate limits or queueing we have no SLA visibility into (pricing isn't even public, per `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §6). Second is Vercel's `maxDuration = 60`: a slow STT poll (20 attempts × 3 s can reach 60 s) plus TTS could exceed the function limit under load, returning a 500 to a patient. Third is cost/abuse: `/api/chat` has no authentication or rate limiting, so the endpoint is open to someone burning our Sahara quota. Mitigations are known and conventional — per-IP rate limiting, a queue with backpressure instead of synchronous polling, and an SLA conversation with Intron — but we'd be dishonest to claim the current build is production-scale.

## Q8: Your privacy claim is "no persistent storage," but you ship patient audio to Intron and text to OpenAI. Isn't that just moving the problem?

Partially, yes — and `docs/ETHICS.md` says so plainly: "audio and transcripts are sent to these processors transiently over TLS." What we control and verified in the code: `package.json` has no database or storage dependency, the API route writes nothing to disk, `console.error` in the catch block logs the error object, and all session state lives in per-request memory that is unrecoverable after the invocation ends. So there is no database to breach, no logs of patient content to subpoena, and nothing to delete because nothing persists — the "Data Deletion" section of the ethics note is trivially satisfied by architecture rather than policy. The residual trust is placed in Intron's and OpenAI's processing terms, which is exactly why the consent modal discloses third-party voice processing before any recording begins and why a text-only path exists for users who decline. A hospital-grade deployment would add a BAA/DPA with Intron and potentially the offline-Whisper fallback already sketched in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §7.

## Q9: Intron had a multi-hour STT/TTS outage during your own benchmark period. What happens to a patient when Sahara is down?

That outage is documented in `docs/BENCHMARK.md` §5 because it forced us to answer this question for real. Today: STT failure throws, the route catches it, and the patient gets a generic 500 — functional but not graceful; the honest gap is that there's no user-facing "voice is temporarily unavailable, please type" fallback banner, though the text-only path (`textChat`) keeps working because it never touches STT. TTS failure is already handled correctly: it's swallowed in a try/catch and the text response is delivered regardless. We also observed Sahara returning empty transcripts for 8/120 real samples (scored WER 1.0) — short or noisy clips the API declined to transcribe — so "Sahara returned nothing" is a case the agent must handle, and currently it would triage an empty string as low urgency, which is the wrong default; flagging empty transcripts for re-record is an obvious fix we haven't shipped. The structural mitigation is the pluggable `SaharaClient` boundary: an offline Whisper fallback for low-connectivity clinics is already named in the benchmark doc as planned, with a visible accuracy caveat.

## Q10: There are no automated tests in this repo. How do you justify that for a patient-facing system?

For a hackathon sprint we made an explicit trade: verification effort went into the benchmark harness and reproducibility artifacts (per-sample JSONL results, kernel script in `scripts/kaggle-benchmark.py`, bundled AfriSwitch subsets in `public/data/`) rather than unit tests — the things judges can actually re-run. That said, the highest-value tests are obvious and small: `detectEmergency` and `computeUrgency` are pure functions that should have golden-case tests per language (including the negation false-positive cases from Q5), and the route's 400/500 branches are trivially testable with a mocked agent. The LangGraph nodes are similarly pure given a stubbed LLM client. We don't claim this is acceptable for production — a real clinical deployment needs a regression suite for the safety net exactly because substring rules are fragile — but the code was structured (pure functions, injectable clients) so that suite can be added without refactoring.

## Q11: Why Next.js + Vercel serverless for a latency-sensitive voice pipeline instead of a dedicated backend?

Pragmatics plus fit. Next.js gave us the UI, API routes, and one-command Vercel deployment in a single TypeScript codebase — critical for a small team on a deadline — and serverless fits the workload: requests are stateless, bursty, and 5–10 s each, with no websockets or long-lived connections required by the current Sahara file-upload API. `force-dynamic` and `maxDuration = 60` in the route reflect that we know we're on serverless and tuned for it. The known costs: cold starts add latency on idle, the 60 s cap bounds how patient we can be with Sahara polling, and if Intron ships a streaming STT API we'd likely need a long-lived server (or Vercel's Fluid/edge streaming) to exploit it. We chose the architecture that ships and demos reliably today over the one that scales hypothetically tomorrow, while keeping the Sahara boundary behind a client class so a backend migration touches one file.

## Q12: Point out the inefficiencies in your own hot path. What would you refactor first?

Several, honestly. (1) Audio travels as base64 inside JSON — a ~33% size inflation plus an encode/decode round trip; multipart upload straight from the browser would be leaner. (2) STT polling sleeps a fixed 3 s between attempts, so results arrive up to 3 s late on average 1.5 s; exponential backoff with a shorter initial interval would shave real latency. (3) `createVoiceAgent` compiles the LangGraph graph on every request — cheap, but it should be module-level. (4) TTS is sequential after the response and returns the WAV as a base64 data URL embedded in JSON instead of a streamed or referenced asset. (5) The emergency and symptom term lists run `fold()` on every term for every request instead of precomputing folded constants. None of these change correctness; all are one-file fixes. We listed them because we think knowing your own hot path's flaws is part of technical execution, not because a judge found them first.

# Judge Q&A — Ethics, Safety & Inclusion (10%) + General/Logistics

## Q1: You claim "no patient data is stored," but audio and transcripts are sent to Intron Sahara and OpenAI. Isn't that just outsourcing the privacy problem?

Partially, yes — and we say so plainly in `docs/ETHICS.md`: audio and transcripts are sent to those processors transiently over TLS. What we control, we lock down: the application itself never writes audio or transcripts to disk, keeps everything in volatile memory, and discards it when the session ends, so there is no database to breach, leak, or subpoena from our side. What we don't control is the retention policy of the upstream processors, and we won't pretend otherwise. For a production deployment the honest next step is a data-processing agreement with Intron (and dropping the OpenAI fallback entirely), plus a published privacy policy. For this prototype, the architecture already minimizes the data footprint to the smallest thing that can function: transient processing, nothing persisted.

## Q2: Your target users are low-literacy patients, yet consent is a written English modal. Isn't that consent theater?

It's a fair criticism and a real limitation. The consent modal (`src/components/voice-ui.tsx:285-306`) currently explains voice processing, temporary handling, and the non-diagnostic nature of the system in English text. We mitigated the access side — a user who declines still gets the full text interface, so consent is genuinely optional and never coerced — but we have not yet solved informed consent in the patient's own language. The credible path is obvious given our own stack: Sahara TTS can speak the consent disclosure in any of the eight supported languages, which is the single first thing we'd add with more time. We flag this as a known gap rather than claiming the current modal is sufficient for the populations we most want to serve.

## Q3: Your emergency "safety net" is roughly 30 keywords in a list. What happens when a patient says "my chest is being crushed" or uses a dialect spelling you didn't list?

Then the deterministic net misses it — that's the honest answer, and it's why the net is designed as a floor, not the whole escalation system. The LangGraph LLM classifier independently assigns urgency, so paraphrased emergencies are still caught semantically; the keyword net (`src/lib/agent.ts:31-48`) exists for the inverse failure, where an LLM misses "siwezi kupumua" (Swahili: I can't breathe) said in a low-resource language. The net fails safe by design: substring matching after ASCII-folding is deliberately over-inclusive, so false positives escalate a non-emergency (cost: an alarmed patient, a red badge) rather than false-negatives suppressing a real one. With more time we'd expand the term lists with clinician review per language and add unit tests with native-speaker phrasings — the current lists are 2–7 terms per language and we know that's thin for Shona and Kinyarwanda especially.

## Q4: Your own benchmark shows Sahara at 0.881 WER on Igbo and 0.885 on Shona. Isn't it irresponsible to offer health triage in languages where the ASR misunderstands most words?

It's the hardest question about this project, and we published those numbers ourselves precisely so it would be asked. Our position: the responsible move is transparency plus gating, not silence. We disclose the per-language breakdown in full (Hausa 0.371, Pidgin 0.390, Swahili 0.400 vs Yoruba 0.688, Igbo 0.881, Shona 0.885 on the N=120 real AfriSwitch run), and the UI language selector plus per-language ASR hints exist because we measured that the wrong hint makes things worse, not better. What we would not do is quietly average those numbers into a "0.603 overall" and ship Igbo triage as if it worked. A production rollout would launch in the proven languages first, keep Igbo/Shona behind an explicit accuracy caveat with a text fallback, and prioritize fine-tuning there — which is exactly the plan we'd carry into the extended challenge.

## Q5: AfriSwitch is scraped from public YouTube videos and podcasts. Did those speakers consent to being used in health-domain benchmarking, and how does the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license constrain you?

The speakers consented to public broadcast, not specifically to benchmark inclusion — that's a known ethical gray zone of in-the-wild datasets, and it sits with the dataset creators (Intron), not with us. On our side, the usage is license-compliant: AfriSwitch is released CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, this is a non-commercial open-source research submission, we attribute the dataset in `docs/ETHICS.md` and the benchmark report, and we redistribute no audio beyond what the license permits. Two hard lines we drew: no real patient data is used anywhere in the benchmark or demo (the demo uses consented self-recordings and Sahara-TTS-generated synthetic samples, labeled as such), and we treat the real AfriSwitch utterances as evaluation data only, never as training data or product content. The NC clause does mean a commercial version of this product would need a separate licensing conversation with Intron — we won't claim otherwise.

## Q6: How does a user exercise a right to deletion or erasure against your system?

There is nothing to delete, by construction. No persistent storage exists: audio, transcripts, and session state live in volatile memory only and are unrecoverable once the session ends — there are no user accounts, no logs of patient content, no analytics on transcripts. That means deletion isn't a request workflow, it's the default state of every session. The one caveat we state honestly: the transient copies processed by Intron Sahara and the optional OpenAI fallback are governed by those providers' own retention policies, outside our control. If we ever added session persistence for clinical continuity, we'd add an explicit deletion endpoint and a retention clock at the same time — privacy architecture first, feature second.

## Q7: What actually stops a frightened user from treating your urgency badge as a medical diagnosis?

Three layers, all in code rather than in a disclaimer page nobody reads. First, the agent schema (`src/lib/agent.ts:14`) hard-codes `not_a_diagnosis: true` on every triage summary, and the UI renders "Not a diagnosis — share with a healthcare professional" directly on every handoff card (`voice-ui.tsx:383`). Second, the system prompt instructs the classifier to never provide definitive diagnoses and to route to a professional, and the consent modal repeats the non-diagnostic framing before the user can record a single second of audio. Third, every recommended action is a routing decision — self-care, telehealth, in-person visit, or seek immediate care — never a named condition or treatment. Could a user still over-trust a "low" badge? Yes, and that's why the emergency path is deterministic and the handoff card is addressed to a human provider, not framed as a final answer.

## Q8: Substring keyword matching is fragile — "breath" matches inside unrelated words, and your net could misfire or miss dialect variants. How robust is this really?

Not as robust as a clinical safety system needs to be, and we've scoped it accordingly: it's a deterministic floor under an LLM classifier, not a certified triage device. The failure modes are asymmetric by design — a false positive (say, "chest" matching an innocent context) produces an over-escalation with a "seek immediate care" message, while the LLM classifier still handles semantic paraphrases the keyword list misses. ASCII-folding (`fold()` in `agent.ts:66`) handles the diacritic-spelling problem across Yoruba, Igbo, and Hausa orthographies, which was the most likely systematic miss. What we have not done is validate the net against a labeled corpus of real emergency utterances per language — that requires clinician-reviewed test sets and is explicitly on the extended-challenge roadmap. We'd rather be judged on a safety net with known limits than on a claim that the LLM alone is safe.

## Q9: Who is the team, and is one person's hackathon prototype an appropriate vehicle for something as safety-critical as medical triage?

The registration is an individual participant (Health category, Nigeria, open-source solution — see `docs/CHALLENGE_RULES.md` registration metadata). On appropriateness: this is a prototype that demonstrates and measures an approach, not a medical device, and nothing in it claims otherwise — no diagnoses, explicit disclaimers, escalation-over-reassurance defaults. The individual scope is also why the engineering choices lean on measurement rather than ambition: instead of promising eight languages, we benchmarked all eight and published which three to four are deployment-ready today. The architecture is deliberately the kind a small team can extend: deterministic safety layers that don't require ML expertise to audit, and a benchmark harness (`/bench`, JSONL artifacts in the repo) that any collaborator can re-run. The path to clinical-grade is a team, native-speaker validation, and regulatory review — the extended challenge is where that starts.

## Q10: How does this carry into the main Sahara CodeSwitch Africa Challenge (through mid-September, winners October 1), and what would you do with more time?

The hackathon deliverable is deliberately a foundation, not a dead end: the 3-model benchmark harness, the per-language WER methodology, and the agent pipeline all generalize. Concrete priorities, ordered by measured need: (1) close the Igbo/Shona/Yoruba gap — fine-tune or evaluate additional ASR options there, since 0.69–0.89 WER is the single biggest measured weakness; (2) clinician-reviewed expansion and validation of the multilingual emergency net, with native-speaker test utterances per language; (3) spoken, in-language consent via Sahara TTS for low-literacy users; (4) the offline/low-bandwidth path the challenge explicitly rewards — the architecture already permits an offline Whisper fallback with a visible accuracy caveat; (5) a data-processing agreement and published privacy policy for real pilot deployments. Every one of those traces to a number or an incident documented in this submission, not to a wish list.

## Q11: You report per-language WER, but what about bias within languages — accents, gender, age, rural vs urban speakers?

We don't measure it yet, and we won't pretend per-language WER is the whole bias story. What exists today: the benchmark evaluates per-language and per-accent WER to surface disparities (`docs/ETHICS.md`, Bias Mitigation), and the AfriSwitch test split is in-the-wild speech — unscripted podcasts and YouTube — so it already contains real accent and noise diversity rather than studio read-speech. What's missing is a demographic slice analysis (gender, age, region) per language; AfriSwitch's public metadata doesn't fully support that breakdown at N=120 without overfitting slices of 10–20 samples. The honest mitigation is methodological: we publish per-sample JSONL results so anyone can re-slice the data, and expanding N per language with demographic metadata is part of the extended-challenge benchmark plan. The one bias finding we do act on today is language-tier disparity, and it's handled by gating and disclosure (Q4).

## Q12: Your consent gate is client-side state that resets every session. Is that real consent or checkbox compliance?

Functionally it's real: `consentGiven` gates the microphone entirely (`voice-ui.tsx:285-308`), declining routes to a fully usable text interface, and the modal discloses processing, transience, and the non-diagnostic nature before any recording. Re-asking each session is deliberate, not a bug — since nothing is persisted, there is no stored consent record to rely on, and re-prompting matches the transient data lifecycle. Where it falls short of gold-standard informed consent is language and comprehension (Q2) and the absence of an auditable consent log, which matters for clinical deployment but is moot while nothing is stored. So: real gating, honest disclosure, documented limitations — and we rank "consent a low-literacy Hausa speaker actually understands" as a top-three follow-up item, not a closed ticket.
